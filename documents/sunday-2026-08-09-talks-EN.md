# FIRST TALK — Public talk: "What Can You Do to Live Forever?"
*10:04–10:35*

**Chairman:** Today we'll be glad to find out the answer to a very interesting question, and I'm
sure we want to know what we must do. The title of the talk is in the form of a question: *What

---

Have you had the opportunity to cultivate the soil? Some sow seeds just to enjoy a few flowers;
others of you have perhaps even planted trees. It's fascinating to work a larger plot of land and
enjoy the fruit of the earth. It's fascinating to put a seed in the ground, water the fertile
soil, and, when the sun's rays kiss the earth, [watch] the seeds germinate, begin to grow, turn
green, and blossom. It is fascinating to watch the miracle of life, following a seed as it
develops and transforms. The special satisfaction, the joy, surpasses all limits when harvest
time comes — when the flower blooms, when you pick the fruit, or when you get to make a loaf of
bread or a sweet bread, when you enjoy the fruit from a tree. At the harvest you can rejoice
greatly — or you can suffer a great disappointment.

What exactly makes the difference between joy and disappointment? I invite you to open the Bible
together to Galatians chapter 6, where we'll read verses 7 and 8. Galatians 6:7, 8: *"Do not be
misled: God is not one to be mocked. For whatever a man sows, this he will also reap; because the
one sowing with a view to his flesh will reap corruption from his flesh, but the one sowing with a
view to the spirit will reap everlasting life from the spirit."*

Therefore, joy or disappointment depends on the seeds sown. "What a man sows, that he will reap"
is an old proverb, born in ancient agricultural societies. But the proverb states a reality — an
unchanging, fundamental principle about our life. The idea it conveys is this: the quality of our
life, in the medium and long term, depends on what we sow in our minds and hearts in the present.

It's true that some people don't think much about the future. Some even say, "Live for the moment.
Enjoy life while you're still young; let come what may — whatever happens to the whole world will
happen to us too." And it's true that sometimes it seems those who don't think much [prosper] —
[but they sow] the bad seeds. In fact, whoever doesn't know any better really can't tell the
difference between quality seeds and the seeds of worthless weeds. In the same way, in the world
we live in, it's ever harder to tell the difference between good and bad; the boundaries between
them are ever harder to identify. But the Bible helps us here and gives us a warning: *Do not be
misled.* We may deceive ourselves; we may deceive others; but God we cannot deceive. Nothing good
can come from a bad seed. Likewise, when a man sows bad things into his life, he won't have good
results in the long run.

So here are two options, in view of the results — because there really are two options: corruption,
or everlasting life. There aren't many. Which would you go for? What would you choose? Obviously
we'd be interested in everlasting life. For some, perhaps, everlasting life is a hard idea to
grasp: if we look around us, people generally live life as they live it, and they all die. But
let's not forget — what we see now is not [ultimate] reality. God did not create us to die. If we
just read from the book of Genesis: God created many wonderful things, many plants — speaking of
seeds — and all vegetation, a paradise on earth. He says, "Let us make man in our image, according
to our likeness." As long as God lives — and God does not die — [so] man was created in the image
and likeness of God, with God's qualities; man was created to reflect God's qualities in his life,
and was created to live forever. Death is an accident. People rebelled; they did not listen to God.
But God did not abandon his purpose.

So in the coming minutes we'll examine three important ideas: What does it mean to sow to the
flesh? — we've seen that sowing to the flesh means corruption and death. What does it mean to sow
to the spirit? — because that leads to everlasting life. And what must we do about it every day?
Because — have you noticed? — you sow something in a pot or in the garden, but if you only come
back in the autumn, you'll reap nothing; you have to tend what you've sown.

## First idea — What it means to sow to the flesh

Let's focus on the first idea. Usually, with a harder-to-understand verse like the one we just read
from Galatians 6:7, 8, it might be hard for someone to grasp. Some say, "Now everyone interprets
the Bible their own way." No — the Bible interprets itself; we analyze the context. Let's go one
chapter back, to chapter 5, verses 19 to 21. Galatians 5:19-21 — what does it mean to sow to the
flesh? *"…strife, divisions, sects, envy, drunkenness, wild parties, and things like these. As to
these things, I am forewarning you, the same way I already forewarned you, that those who practice
such things will not inherit God's kingdom."*

So what are the works of the flesh? I don't have to give any definition; I don't have to explain
anything to you, because the Bible says they are clearly visible. How many are there? I counted
fifteen. Satan has made a powerful team. Through these things he draws people away from God and
leads them to death, to destruction — what he has done ever since the garden of Eden. But is it
only fifteen? These are the ones we focus on, but the expression "and things like these" also
appears. Just as a football team has eleven good players — the goalkeeper and ten who run — but any
respectable team also has a player on the bench, so Satan too has a player on the bench. If a
person doesn't resist these inclinations, and others like them, he can end up sinning. At first
they are in the form of seeds. You saw that envy and jealousy were mentioned. You think, "I haven't
done anything wrong yet" — but if these things are already developing in the mind and heart, there
was already a risk of our drawing away from God.

Traits are developing in us that can lead us to corruption, to death. What does corruption mean?
Degradation. But degradation doesn't mean someone who made a mistake and — bam — you see him
explode, disintegrating. How is it with plants? Rotting doesn't start visibly: a fruit looks good,
but when you want to eat it you find it doesn't taste good, or inside it has begun to rot. So it is
with a man: he degrades spiritually — he no longer resembles God — or he degrades morally, and
manifestations appear in his life that are not right. Moral degradation begins when you practice
such things, and then physical degradation follows — that is, death. That's why it says those who
practice such things have no [everlasting] life. We won't dwell on the bad things — you see them at
every turn: you go on the internet, you watch TV. These are the dangerous things. What interests us
is how to sow with a view to everlasting life.

## Second idea — What it means to sow to the spirit

What does it mean to sow to the spirit? We have it in Galatians 5:22, 23. Let's read together:
*"On the other hand, the fruitage of the spirit is [love, joy, peace, patience…]."* We all like to
be in the presence of loving, joyful, peaceable, patient people who exercise self-control in hard
circumstances. We like gentle people, don't we? So where do we find them? How can we too become
such people? Thanks to the holy spirit — when we form a friendship with God, the better we get to
know him and become fascinated by his magnificent personality.

You can [come to know] God even by looking at creation. We started with flowers, plants, fields.
When you look at it the way you look at a beautiful painting and understand what kind of person the
painter is — you look at planet Earth, at the mountains, the oceans, the seas — you understand what
God is like. And then we have the Bible, which reveals God to us. It also answers other questions:
What is God's name? What is the purpose of life? Why did he give us life? What does he expect of us?

And when we want to change our personality, let's not say, "This is just me; I was born this way and
this is how I want to die." We were born in sin, and we don't want to die. That's the idea — and if
we want life, we want to choose life, not death. It's important to know God, to connect with him,
to develop these qualities in our lives. It's true that some of these qualities are visible in the
majority of people — because we are created in God's image and likeness. Some even display them to
a greater degree: for example, if I grew up in a family that gave me a fine upbringing, if our
parents took good care of us, or if others went to university, did a master's, or work in a field
and move in an environment where such qualities are valued — some even pursue personal development
and refine their character.

But here we're not talking about refining character. A person who behaves nicely in favorable
conditions can disappoint deeply in times of crisis — personal disappointments, social or political
conflicts, wars. For example, Sibiu and this part of Transylvania is beautiful partly thanks to the
German influence — the Saxons. In other regions you don't find fortified churches like these and
such distinctive buildings, and that order, that discipline. It's true, we appreciate quality,
educated people, as the Germans are — we even have guests from Germany today; there's more order
there than in Romania, perhaps, and it's cleaner. But what did many Germans, with their refined
character, do when Hitler came to power with his National Socialist party? When Nazism was in
power, what did the most refined people in that empire do? They forgot their norms and principles
and gladly killed millions of people. Who carried out the genocide? Who killed about six million
Jews and many other nations? So refining character is not enough. If a man, however good he may be,
has no connection with God — doesn't do good from God's standpoint, doesn't take divine standards
into account…

Letting the holy spirit act — resembling God in spirit — means knowing God well and praying for
holy spirit, because by our own strength we cannot rise to the level of divine standards. But if we
pray fervently to the true God, Jehovah, he can help us through the holy spirit to develop not just
some qualities, not just civilized behavior, but [something] deep in our hearts, so that we truly
want to behave [rightly] and to love our fellow man. In the end, no one has the right to kill
another human being — did *you* give anyone life? And when you come to think like God, then you can
say you resemble him in spirit.

Some people even tell us, "Maybe you've been to other churches, but you Jehovah's Witnesses have
the Holy Spirit, because we've received the Holy Spirit and are saved." We rejoice when someone has
received holy spirit — but "the Holy Spirit" and "holy spirit" are the same thing; Catholics say
one word, the Orthodox another, but it's the same. And it's not like a parcel you receive when the
courier comes — you paid, you got your parcel, and it's yours. Holy spirit isn't like that. We need
the actions of the holy spirit every single day, in every aspect of our lives.

## Third idea — What we must do every day

The moment we connected with God, we received holy spirit within us. In our minds and hearts a war
begins, a battle. We become aware — we know what pleases God, and what we should do to live
forever, because in the Bible the information is clear. But the heart is treacherous; it pulls at
us, and a battle begins within us. What can we do to win this fight between good and evil that goes
on in our minds and hearts? We've come to three [sub-]ideas.

Please, engineer, [display] the image. Let's open to Romans 8:5, 6. How can we make sure we sow, in
our minds, the seeds that lead to everlasting life? *"For those who live according to the flesh set
their minds on the things of the flesh, but those who live according to the spirit, on the things
of the spirit. For setting the mind on the flesh means death, but setting the mind on the spirit
means life and peace."* So on what does success — a rich harvest, and happiness — depend? It
matters a great deal what we direct our minds toward. All information enters our minds, our brains,
through the sight of our eyes. It matters a lot what you look at.

Here's an image: if this man looks at violent images… You see how many films there are now with a
lot of violence, and the hero beats [others] to be the strongest, the best. You think, "Well, I
watch action films — it's just entertainment; I'll never go and do something like that." Fine — but
what are you sowing in your mind? You develop certain traits. For instance, at some point, at his
workplace, he no longer likes the boss giving him directions. If a man feeds himself this way,
always watching such films, he can reach the point of believing he's better than his boss, and it
even annoys him when the boss gives advice or sets him a work schedule. You develop a self-centered
character — that's what you're sowing.

Or, if I looked at immoral images, what would develop in the mind, in the heart? At first you may
say, "I'd never do such a thing." It's like with a seed: when you put it in a pot, at first you see
nothing, it's all soil, as if you'd done nothing; but in time it will sprout. So it is with bad
things — at first you just see them, but gradually certain selfish, immoral, destructive desires
develop in our minds and hearts. That's why, when violent or immoral images appear before us
without our wanting them — images of degradation, of drugs — I'm not ashamed to say out loud, the
way football fans cheer when their team scores at the World Cup: "I don't want to sow that in my
mind; I don't want to sow that in my heart." I'm not ashamed to say it even out loud, because you
don't want to ruin your life.

Instead, if we fill our minds with the truth from God's Word — if we study the Bible, if we go to
the website jw.org, where we find only spiritual information (you have materials, videos, the film
about Jesus) — if you fill your mind with spiritual values, then something develops in the mind and
heart: the desire to share them with others, the good news of the Kingdom — the desire to tell
others about everlasting life, about the new world, about paradise. It doesn't mean you'll be rid
of problems — at work you've still got the boss; the boss is the boss — but your attitude, if
you've fed your mind with spiritual things, [is different]: "Yes, boss, you're always right; I'll
do as you say." A humble attitude — because in the end you'll do better that way, and you promote
peace.

Also — please, engineers — let's go to the second image. Some people really are disappointed by
what they're fed in films, on TV. Recently a young woman told me, "Brother, [I'm surprised] you
watch TV." I said, "Well, I used to watch those matches — the World Cup — and I watch the news to

That's right. Now, it's not that you only find negative things on social media, and "don't look at

posted: where they're eating, what holidays they're taking, their new purchases, what car they've
bought. At first glance, nothing bad. As far as I'm concerned, may everyone live well — eat at

But is this real life? Let's be realistic. Even we, when we took photos this week — at the field

But what would happen if I spent a lot of time admiring other people's posts? [Take] the garden: if
my neighbor has a gorgeous garden — flowers, roses, trees bending under their fruit — and mine is a
ruin, how does it help me to stare at his garden? "Wow, what a beautiful garden" — that doesn't

the same with life: if I keep looking at other people's lives, I get depressed. "Wow, look how good

You see, when a drought comes, if you don't water the garden, everything dries up. We all go
through hard moments in life. Just as the householder waters the garden in the hard times of
drought, each of us has the responsibility to water the soil of the heart. And we have, in God's
Word, enough living water — the water of life — with which to water the soil of our heart, so that
those qualities we spoke of may flourish. Others' success does not represent our failure. Envy and
jealousy don't help us — they are works of the flesh that lead to degradation. If you look at
others a lot and become envious and jealous, you only hurt yourself. So I have no choice but to
work carefully on my mind and heart — to take care how I cultivate the fruit of the holy spirit,
removing the works of the flesh from the garden of my life.

And, in addition, image number three. Please, [water] engineer — one more image. Notice: if this

Does any of you have a problem? Those who have no problems… [be assured] that every problem can be
solved. All problems are temporary. The problem is not the problems. We all have problems from time
to time; and if we don't have them, we will. It's not the problems that are the problem — rather,
the point is not to become slaves to our problems.

In the coming minutes we'll learn from the example of someone who, at first glance, shouldn't have
had problems, but whose life in the end was hard and difficult. We'll speak only about Joseph. Who
was Joseph? He was the eleventh son of Israel — of Jacob, who was a patriarch. So Joseph was born
into a family of worshippers of Jehovah — a wealthy family. Please, let's open to Genesis chapter
37. Even Joseph had some problems — big problems. Where did they begin? Genesis 37:3, 4: *"Israel
loved Joseph more than all his other sons, because he was the son of his old age, and he had a
special robe made for him. When his brothers saw that their father loved him more than them, they
began to hate him, and they could not speak peaceably to him."*

For a teenager, for a young person, this can be a very big problem — and even for adults, we don't
like it when others hate us, when they treat us with hostility. When a stranger speaks to you that
way, [it's one thing]; but when such problems arise within your own family — your own brothers
hating you — and when it says they could no longer speak well to him, it means they spoke to him
harshly, insulted him, treated him abusively. Think about it — it wasn't pleasant at all. What a
life the young man Joseph had! And, as if that weren't enough, there was this tense atmosphere in
the family. At one point Joseph's brothers went with the animals, with the flocks — for they were
wealthy — to a more distant region, and Jacob said to him, "Joseph, take some provisions, get
dressed, and go see how your brothers are doing." Said and done — Joseph went, obedient to his
father. And notice, in Genesis 37:19, 20, what happens: they saw Joseph from a distance — how his
robe was special, how well he was dressed — and they said to one another, "Look, here comes the
dreamer. Let's kill him and throw him into a waterpit; we'll say a wild animal devoured him, and
then we'll see what becomes of his dreams." And they didn't just say it — they didn't merely think
such a thing. Verse 24: *"They took him and threw him into the waterpit. The pit was empty at the
time; there was no water in it."*

How is that — your own brothers, whom you go to see how they're doing, instead of embracing and
encouraging you — "our dear little brother, how are you? how is Dad?" — instead of affection,
instead of love, they seize him and throw him into a pit. He would have been drowned had there been
water — a good thing there was none. And that wasn't all: since he hadn't died in the pit, they
could have left him there to die. Then Judah had an idea: "Why should he die for nothing? Why don't
we make some money?" They saw a caravan of merchants passing by and said, "Let's do it — we'll get
rid of him and earn some money too." Said and done: they pulled him out of the pit, sold him to the
merchants, and Joseph ends up a slave in Egypt.

You know the account. Some of us grew up with it — it was our favorite story; when our parents told
it to us at night, we'd fall asleep to this beautiful account. But we'll draw three important
lessons. I know none of you escapes problems — some of you have had big problems in life; others,
right now, are going through some difficulty. Three lessons from Joseph for when you go through
hardships, sorrows, injustices:

1. **Do not let yourself be overcome by bitterness.**
2. **Do everything that depends on you.**
3. **Wait on Jehovah.**

Let's see how the Bible highlights these three valuable choices.

## Lesson 1 — Do not fall prey to resentment

Joseph becomes a slave, sold in Egypt to Potiphar, another of Pharaoh's officials. As a slave
there, he could have filled himself with bitterness — "What they did to me!" — and developed [such]
feelings. But if we read the account, we see that Joseph, in reality, worked and minded his
business. The years pass, and Joseph comes into a favorable situation. He is very successful in
Potiphar's house too; in the end Potiphar even puts his whole house and wealth under Joseph's
management, and Joseph enjoyed special privileges. Then, after an extremely negative event — we'll
come back to it — he even ends up held in a maximum-security prison. And [later] Joseph becomes, in
effect, the prime minister of Egypt, the right hand of Pharaoh. People were starving, and Joseph
managed Egypt's grain with such wisdom — he had huge storehouses. And so Joseph now enjoyed special
privileges.

Then Joseph's brothers come. Jacob had said to them, "Why do you sit here while we die of hunger?
Get on the animals — we have money — go to Egypt; I've heard there's enough grain there. Get
provisions so we survive." Said and done: they come to Egypt, and who do they end up buying grain
from? Please, engineers, display image number one. There is Joseph. He recognizes his brothers
immediately and questions them, interrogates them: "Where do you come from? What do you want? [Is
your father] still alive? Do you have parents?" "Yes, we have a father." "How many brothers are
you?" "There were ten of us; we sold one, and we still have one — Benjamin, the youngest." "All
right then." Done — Joseph says, "Fill the sacks." He gave them as much grain as they wanted, and
[had] the money put back in the sacks, and sends them off. When these people, on the way, see that
all the silver was back in the sacks, they turn around: "It can't be — but we paid just now."
Actually, Joseph is testing them; he wants to see whether they've become honest.

He interrogates them again — over three episodes — and tells them, "Go, and bring your youngest
brother." He puts them in a situation similar to the one when they sold Joseph. He knew very well
that Jacob loved his youngest sons — Joseph, whom he no longer had, and Benjamin. When Joseph says,
"Bring Benjamin; take your grain, here's your money back, go and see to your lives," even Judah —
the very one who had come up with the idea, "Let's make some money off Joseph" — says, "No; take
me. I'll become your slave; I'll do whatever you want, but not Benjamin." Then Joseph was convinced
that his brothers were no longer the same as before. He saw that these men had repented, had made
progress; they were honest and upright, and bitterly regretted what they had done years earlier.

They didn't recognize him — clean, groomed, shaved, perfumed — whereas they were the same shepherds
with their animals. But what do we see in Joseph at this moment? Could he now be full of bitterness
and jealousy? Think about it: he now holds in his hand those who had all but killed him, who sold
him, who robbed him of the most beautiful years of his life — his adolescence, the years when a
person is healthy, happy, full of imagination, energy, and talent. How much could Joseph have done
in those years, while he lay enslaved, and then in prison? Could he now take revenge, hold them to
account? But Joseph did nothing of the sort. He didn't freeze their image in time. He doesn't look
at what his brothers did; he chooses to forgive them. He sees their repentance, he sees their
potential. Joseph understands that these are the men from whom a nation can rise.

When others hurt you badly — see how easily we can fill up with bitterness. Yet if we let ourselves
be dominated by negative feelings, we may do ourselves more harm than others have done us. Our
negative feelings, the bitterness in our heart, can rob us of happiness. Sometimes a person who
offended us, who hurt us, doesn't even realize it. There are people — like the ones we just analyzed
at the Watchtower study — who are refined, who speak beautifully, who are very attentive and
principled, "all of a piece" — yet one of them does something to you such that you can't sleep for
nights on end, turning from side to side; they've robbed you of your sleep, your peace, and it
crushes you: "What could this one do to me!" — while he has no problem; he sleeps soundly. So the
problem is not him; the problem is me — the feelings in my heart, the bitterness developing in my
heart. "I want to get justice for myself." You want justice — you, in a world where justice is
broken, in Satan's system?

We don't want to become victims of our own problems. Yes, when we suffer, it's a problem; but we
don't want to become slaves to our problems. Sometimes the problem is a single one — we can leave
it behind, we can forgive, we can let it all go and move forward joyfully, convinced that Jehovah
has seen it and that Jehovah is the God of justice. If you bear no grudge, you've done your part;
you can be at peace. But if we dwell too much on the wrong — if we think too much about it — we
become slaves not to one problem but to many; because when a person dwells on a wrong, he starts
making up scenarios: "How could so-and-so do that to me? Just wait — I'll meet him, and here's what
I'll say, here's what I'll do" — and maybe you tell others too, "Look what he did to me." "I'll go
to the congregation elders"; and if the elders don't give him justice, "the overseer will come";
and if the overseer "doesn't give me justice either — I'll write to the branch"; and if the branch
doesn't, "I'll write to the Governing Body." You had a single problem — but if you fill yourself
with bitterness, you can become the slave of many. You breed problems for others, and you suffocate
under a mountain of problems.

Let's keep this idea: Joseph chose to forgive. He wept. Notice how he proceeded, in Genesis
45:3-5 — the lesson for us. His brothers were arguing among themselves, "What have we done? Now God
is punishing us on account of our brother," caught there as if in a vise. And Joseph weeps; he
pities them; he has a longing for them. He still hadn't told them who he was — he would have
embraced them, but he let them see how they [really] were — and then he tells them: *"Please come
closer."* And they came near. Then he said, *"I am Joseph, your brother, whom you sold into Egypt.
But now do not be worried, and do not blame one another for selling me here, for God sent me ahead
of you to preserve life."*

Stay focused on the positive side of things. "I can't change the past, but people change." Even the
worst people, who have upset and hurt us — see how Joseph's brothers changed! All people can change.
But I don't change them by answering with the same coin — you don't change anyone if you too become
malicious; I don't change them by taking revenge. The only way to change things is to break the
vicious cycle of malice — through love, through forgiveness, through kindness, as Joseph did, as
other people of faith have done.

## Lesson 2 — Do everything that depends on you

When you go through problems, do everything that depends on you. What did Joseph do? Notice Genesis
39:2: *"Jehovah was with Joseph, so he became successful,"* and he received responsibilities in the
house of his Egyptian master. Instead of being full of bitterness, what did he do? He kept a good
relationship with Jehovah, and he learned to manage goods, learned about agriculture and economics;
he paid the workers, and Potiphar's business prospered — he had everything in hand. Jehovah was
with him. What can we too do when, instead of complaining that we don't have the ideal situation, we
do our part anyway?

Please, engineer, display the image from the prison. We know you're thinking, "Well —" [but] do
good, because he did good. After that, when Potiphar's wife — living well, growing ever richer —
set her eyes on him and wanted a sexual affair, he refused her: "How could I do such a thing?"
First of all he had respect for Jehovah — "I fear God" — and then, toward his master too: "I want
to be honest and upright." And because he was honest and upright, Potiphar did not believe Joseph
but believed his immoral wife, and condemned him and threw him into prison. Yes — sometimes, you
know how it is, you do good and it turns out badly. Joseph could have said, "So this is what I get
for doing good — and now I've ended up in prison," a very hard life. But instead of Joseph sitting
on the floor with his morale on the ground, weeping in self-pity, full of bitterness — no: he's the
one who distributes food to the prisoners! Whether as a slave at Potiphar's or in prison, Joseph
got to work and made [everything] depend on him: he swept, he cleaned, he managed the money, he
increased his master's wealth; in prison he got to work and encouraged the other prisoners, so that
at one point the prison administrator says, "Here — you manage the prison; you do it better than I
do."

How is it at school? For some, school is their prison. Do you like it, or not? Sometimes you think,
"I'm not going to school anymore." But just as you can't leave prison when you want, you can't leave
school either until you pass your final exam — and if you drop out of school, you'll have even more
problems. You have a problem — fight it; but dropping out will bring a mountain of problems, because
in life you'll always need at least a minimum qualification to make the most of yourself. So even if
school is your prison, do everything that depends on you there. Not everyone can be brilliant; not
everyone can be an Olympian; some can't learn well — for some, math is hard, for others Romanian.
But can you at least behave nicely toward your teachers, toward the other children? You can speak
nicely to your teachers. You don't like math? You can bring the math teacher a flower, or sweets
now and then, a chocolate. You don't like that subject? But you can be honest and upright, and stand
out among the others through other qualities. It depends on you.

Do you like it at work? "I can't wait for Sunday to pass — tomorrow morning, off to work." For some,
the workplace is their prison. Some say, "I'm just not going to work tomorrow." Fine — and if you
don't go, will it be better? You have a problem at work; but if you have no job, you'll have more
problems. The idea is: when you have a problem, don't become a slave to your own problems. You have
a problem — but when you multiply them yourself, then you become their slave. What can you do? Do
what depends on you, like Joseph. All right, you don't much like that workplace; but you can behave
better toward your boss, and do your work — whether you sweep the floor, work in programming, are
active, do accounting, or handle public relations — make your work a matter of pride, raise it to
the level of art, do quality work. Not the way some say, "It's not worth it — I'm just making him
rich." No — you do it as for Jehovah, not for men. If people don't appreciate it — well, Joseph
wasn't much appreciated by Potiphar either, and he ended up in prison; but in prison he did what he
could, and in the end even there his life became a bit better, because he made things better for
himself.

At your workplace, maybe you don't like it; but if you persevere, stick with the work, and cultivate
better relationships with those around you — you've seen that over time we now have brothers and
sisters who reach retirement age and the boss says, "Please don't leave — come back even after
retirement and help us," and the man, though he has his pension, still goes and draws a salary too,
and [the boss] says, "Come, help train these young ones." Yes — perseverance is rewarded, because
you specialize at a job; however hard it is, if you stay longer, little by little it brings you
satisfaction, appreciation, value, and self-respect. Do your part; do everything that depends on
you. Don't keep dwelling on [others'] reactions. Jehovah cherishes [your effort] and it will bring
you blessings. Set yourself on solutions, not on problems — there are always solutions to the
problems that arise.

## Lesson 3 — Wait on Jehovah

Let's go to the third idea: wait on Jehovah. Engineer, please display our image. Joseph doesn't look
at the damp walls — [imagine] others in that cell, upset, full of bitterness, looking for the rats,
the dust, the filth; Joseph looked toward the heart, toward [Jehovah]. He waited on him; he didn't
turn his back. Joseph understood one thing: people have disappointed us and will disappoint us. Our
life can change from one day to the next, as it did for him — from a little prince he ended up in a
pit; from the pit, a slave; from a slave, a prisoner. Problems come; they come over us; they strike
us with force in this world. Today you're healthy; tomorrow you learn you have a catastrophic
illness. Today you have a job — you're valuable, precious; tomorrow you wake up without a job.
Anything can happen in this unstable system. Today you have a house; a fire comes, a missile, a
bomb. What is certain? Jehovah's love is certain. All problems are temporary — but Jehovah does not
change; problems appear, come and go, but Jehovah's love is eternal.

Let's look to Jehovah; let's not betray him; let's not turn our backs on him; let's not compromise;
and when we have problems, let's not add more. Concretely, for example: you're discouraged because
you haven't yet found a suitable marriage mate. Sometimes a young person feels this — it's normal,
at some point, to think, "I'd like to marry, to have a beautiful family," especially since we see
beautiful, happy families in the congregation and in the world. But don't forget there are also
happy married people — and don't forget there are also many unhappy married people, just as there
can be [happy] single people. The point is not to become a slave to more problems by breaking
Jehovah's standards. Stay like Joseph, who said, "No, I don't do that kind of thing." Reject the
temptation to break Jehovah's command about marrying "only in the Lord." For example, some wanted
so badly, so desperately, to marry — hoping they'd be happy once married — and they compromised:
they married an unsuitable, unbelieving person, or someone they didn't yet really know, and then
they had a problem, after which came [being] slaves to many problems.

Or others are married — but none of us has the marriage of perfect happiness. Don't you have
marriage problems? Yes — even in the happiest marriages, incompatibilities and dissatisfactions
appear. We husbands are sometimes dissatisfied with our wives — but wives too have reasons to
complain about us men sometimes. But don't let yourself be influenced by the spirit of the world,
which encourages separation and divorce. Fight — you and your partner — for your marriage; fight
together to overcome the problems and difficulties. Breaking Jehovah's standards cannot bring you
happiness. That is — some think, "Well, the world says so: we don't get along, the relationship is
toxic, we're separating, each going his own way; each will find happiness with someone else." And
if you were married and you give up on the partner you built and raised [a family] with… But if you
neglected the marriage for a while — like anything else; if you neglect a flower or a car, problems
arise — then the blame is always shared; it's never said that only the wife or only the husband is
at fault. When a problem arises, remember: you have a problem, but don't become a slave to more
problems, to a mountain of them — because separation and divorce lead to other problems. Then
[comes] getting together with other people: "I have my child, he has his child, then we'll have our
children, and then their children and ours, and the assets, and the ex-husband…" — you know how the
problems pile up like mountains, and you'll be crushed.

Simplify your life. Simply put, we have standards, we have principles, we have collaborators, we
have elders who support us, just like Joseph. It matters greatly to wait on Jehovah. We do our part,
and Jehovah helps us; he loves us very much. Is it hard for you to provide for your family because
of financial problems? Waiting on Jehovah means not getting involved in questionable, illegal
activities to earn money. The world offers so many "opportunities": "everyone will give you money,
no credit needed." Fine — but when you [need to] make changes in life — for example, to start
pioneering when you have financial problems — don't go looking for another job, don't rush off
abroad; start pioneering. Go into the preaching ministry, live on less; and when you seek out Bible
studies, a better job will come to find you — because an employer says, "Sir, do you know a good
electrician? I need one. I need a plumber, an IT technician, an administrator." "Well, I have an
offer." And usually the good offers come to the one who puts the Kingdom first — the one to whom
Jehovah says, "I know what you need" — and the problems will be solved. Let's not be blinded by
feelings; we do what depends on us.

I'm looking now a little at those on Zoom. Your prison may even be your own home, and you can feel
sad, downcast: "How I used to go out and pioneer, how beautiful it was — we even went out to the
villages in the ministry — and now I sit here at home. I'd have liked to be there with you at the
program, but I'm on Zoom." A good thing it's possible, through video conference. Maybe your home
becomes your prison — but instead of filling yourself with bitterness and self-pity, do everything
that depends on you there at home. There's video conferencing; you'll make a phone call; you can
witness to the nurse who comes to give you your treatment, your injections; witness to your
grandchildren, to the neighbors. Do what depends on you; do your part — and wait on Jehovah.

In Joseph's case, Jehovah turned those bitter trials into blessings that neither he, nor his whole
family, nor all his brothers, nor Jacob the great patriarch, ever imagined. Be convinced, brothers
and sisters, young or old, that in your case too he can turn the bitterest trials into the sweetest
blessings.

My beloved and lovely wife and I have become convinced that you are the finest people. You are
wonderful; you are phenomenal. Jehovah cherishes you very much. You are like diamonds, well along in
the polishing — we all still have changes to make, but Jehovah cherishes us so much, and he is
preparing us for the new world. When we go through certain trials, he could spare us, but he knows
we learn best [that way]. Sometimes, going through problems, we need certain life lessons; and
Jehovah can hardly wait — the faster you learn and accept the changes, the faster you accept the new
circumstances you find yourself in, the faster you'll regain joy and happiness. Don't resist; don't
fill yourself with bitterness; don't try to get justice for yourself. Wait on Jehovah, and the
results will always be wonderful. From tomorrow morning, we begin, determined.

As for the pioneer school — you have fine representatives; they'll represent you with honor at the
school. Next week we'll delight in [the reports from] the school. We'll be expecting you here at the
break, so drop by to see your fellow workers. Then, after the school, we'll visit the Alba Iulia
Nord congregation — do you agree to send them your love and greetings? Thank you for your support in
every respect. You are wonderful. Now we conclude the program by singing from the heart song [133,
"You Give Me Strength, Hope, Confidence"], after which we'll thank Jehovah in prayer.

*(There followed the closing song and the concluding prayer.)*
